# Stretches

This document is the content and the rules for the Stretches screen: what it asks, what it shows, and where every line comes from. The screen is a short, honest routine of gentle movement for the places people commonly ache, written for someone who has never stretched on purpose. It implements the numbered decisions below and nothing else. Every routine and every warning carries a bracketed source number that points to the Sources section.

Tare does not diagnose, treat, or prescribe. These are the same general exercises health services publish for the public; a routine here is a place to start, not a plan for an injury. Anything that looks like more than an ache gets sent to a clinician, and the screen says so before it shows a single move.

Items marked UNVERIFIED were not confirmed against a primary source at the time of writing and must not be promoted to fact without one.

## Decisions

### The screen

1. The screen asks two questions and then shows one routine. "What hurts?" offers seven places: Lower back, Neck, Shoulders, Hips, Knees, Feet and heels, Hands and wrists. "How?" offers three answers: "Stiff, after sitting or sleeping", "Sore, after doing something", and "Sharp, sudden, or after a fall". The third answer never shows a routine; it shows the Get help card for that place and stops.
   Why: a sudden or sharp pain, or one that follows a fall, is the pattern every source treats as a reason to be seen rather than stretched. [1][3][4][5][6][7] The other two answers describe the aches that gentle movement is published for.

2. Before any routine, the screen shows the Get help card for the chosen place: the signs that mean call 911, the signs that mean see a clinician today, and the point at which an ache that has not improved should be looked at. The card is short, plain, and cannot be skipped the first time a place is opened; after that it sits at the top of the routine as one line, "When to get help", that opens the card.
   Why: the services that publish these exercises put the warning signs beside them, not behind them. [1][3][4][5][6][7][8] Showing the card first is the honest order.

3. Every routine has four to six moves. Each move is a name, plain steps, how long to hold or how many times, one sentence on what it is for, and one line on when to stop that move. No anatomy words in the steps: "the back of your thigh", never "hamstring"; "the muscle at the back of your lower leg", never "gastrocnemius". Where a source names a muscle, the name goes in this document, not on the screen.
   Why: the audience note that shapes every Tare screen. A person who has never tracked or trained anything should be able to read a move once and do it.

4. There is no logging, no streak, no reminder and no timer in this version. The screen is something to read and do, and it remembers only which place was opened last. A move-by-move timer is a later decision.
   Why: his 2026-09-02 call for the round ("no logging in v1"), and the no-medals, no-XP rule that runs through the app.

5. Stretches is a row under More and a row on the desktop rail, with the lucide `StretchHorizontal` glyph, never a bar tab.
   Why: the 2026-09-02 navigation decision (More is the home of every screen that is not one of the four tabs).

### How much, how often

6. A stretch is held for 20 to 30 seconds, to the point of a gentle pull and never to pain, and repeated two to three times. A movement or strengthening move is done five to ten times, slowly. A routine is done two to three times a day when the ache is fresh, and at least two or three days a week after that.
   Why: the ACSM position stand on exercise for healthy adults recommends holding a static stretch 10 to 30 seconds, 2 to 4 repetitions, about 60 seconds per muscle group in total, on 2 to 3 days a week or more, to the point of slight discomfort or tightness. [2] NHS Inform's physiotherapy pages for back and knee problems say to hold stretches 20 to 30 seconds, do 2 to 3 sets, 2 to 3 times a day, and to start movement exercises at 2 to 3 repetitions and add one or two every few days. [9][10] Tare uses the NHS Inform numbers because they are written for a person with an ache, and they sit inside the ACSM range.

7. The pain rule the screen shows, once, above every routine: "Rate the ache from 0 to 10. Up to 5 while you move is fine. Above that, do fewer, go slower, or rest longer between moves. You should be no worse the next morning. Stop the routine if it makes the ache worse or brings a new pain."
   Why: this is NHS Inform's own rule for its physiotherapy exercises, restated in plain words: 0 to 3 minimal, 4 to 5 acceptable, 6 to 10 excessive; stop if symptoms worsen or new pain appears; the morning-after test. [9][10]

8. Most aches ease on their own. The routine's closing line says when to be seen if it has not: "Most aches ease within a few weeks. If this one is no better after six weeks, or it keeps coming back, see a clinician." For hips, shoulders and heels the number is two weeks, per the source for that place.
   Why: NHS Inform says to talk to a healthcare professional if a back problem has not improved within six weeks of following the advice. [9] The Chartered Society of Physiotherapy says most muscle and joint problems get better within six to eight weeks. [11] NHS says to see a GP for hip pain and heel pain not improved after two weeks of self-care, and for shoulder pain that persists beyond two weeks. [5][6][7]

9. Being active is part of every routine's advice, in one sentence: "Keep moving. Walking is as good as any exercise here, and bed rest makes a back worse." Nothing on the screen suggests a belt, a brace, or a gadget.
   Why: NICE's guideline for low back pain and sciatica says to encourage people to continue with normal activities, to consider exercise programmes, and not to offer belts, corsets, foot orthotics, rocker sole shoes or traction. [12] NHS says stay active and do not stay in bed for long periods. [1]

### Warnings

10. The Get help card is written in US words: "Call 911", "See a clinician today", "See a clinician". Tare never names an emergency number from another country. The signs themselves come from the NHS pages, which publish them in three tiers (999, urgent, GP); Tare keeps the three tiers.
    Why: the members are in the United States. The NHS pages are the clearest public source for the tiers and are cited as such.

11. The all-places emergency line appears on every card first: "Call 911 if the pain comes with chest pain or pressure, trouble breathing, sudden weakness or numbness, trouble speaking, or a sudden severe headache, or if it follows a serious accident."
    Why: chest pain and pain after a serious accident are on the NHS 999 list for back pain, [1] chest pain or breathlessness with shoulder pain is on the NHS shoulder urgent list, [5] and the CSP lists new severe headaches, coordination and balance changes among its warning signs. [11] The stroke signs (sudden weakness or numbness, trouble speaking) are general emergency knowledge; their inclusion here is a product decision and the wording is UNVERIFIED against a single cited page.

12. Anyone with diabetes who has foot or heel pain is told to see a clinician rather than start the routine.
    Why: NHS lists diabetes with heel pain as a reason to see a GP. [7]

### Wording

13. The screen uses "clinician" where the sources say GP, doctor, or physiotherapist, and "ache" where they say pain, except in the Get help card, where "pain" stays.
    Why: "GP" is a British word; "clinician" covers a doctor, a nurse practitioner, or a physical therapist without choosing for the member.

14. No move is called a "stretch" for a muscle that is being strengthened; those are called moves. The screen's name stays Stretches because that is the word people use.

## The routines

Each place below lists the Get help card, then the routine. Hold and repetition numbers follow decision 6 unless the source gives its own, in which case the source's number is used and cited.

### Lower back

Get help. Call 911: the all-places line; also numbness or loss of feeling around your genitals or buttocks, new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels, or pain, tingling, weakness or numbness in both legs. [1] See a clinician today: you feel hot, cold, shivery or generally unwell with it, or the pain started suddenly and is getting worse quickly. [1] See a clinician: it is no better after a few weeks of this, it stops you doing your day, you have lost weight without trying, there is a lump or swelling in your back, or it is worse at night or does not ease with rest. [1] NICE lists cancer, infection, trauma and inflammatory disease as the serious causes a clinician rules out. [12]

Routine (five moves, lying on a bed or the floor, then hands and knees): [9]
1. Knee rolls. Lie on your back, knees bent and pointing at the ceiling, feet flat. Slowly roll both knees to the right, hold a few seconds, bring them back up, then roll to the left. That is one. Do five to ten. For: loosening the lower back from side to side.
2. Pelvic tilts. Lie on your back with your hands on your hips. Slowly tilt your hips so your lower back flattens into the bed, hold two seconds, then tilt the other way so a small gap opens under your lower back. Relax. That is one. Do five to ten. For: getting the lower back moving gently.
3. Single knee hug. Lie on your back. Bring one knee up towards your chest, the other knee still pointing at the ceiling. Hold 20 to 30 seconds, then release slowly. Two to three times each side. For: the lower back and the front of the hip.
4. Double knee hug. Lie on your back and slowly bring both knees towards your chest; your hands can rest over the knees to feel it a little more. Hold 20 to 30 seconds, release slowly. Two to three times. For: the lower back.
5. Cat and camel. On hands and knees, hands under your shoulders, knees a little apart. Gently arch your back up towards the ceiling and tuck your chin, then slowly let your back sink, push your chest towards the floor and look gently up. Hold each end a few seconds. Do five to ten. For: the whole spine, in both directions.

Stiff after sitting or sleeping adds: "Small amounts often beat one long session. Two or three of each move every hour or so is a good start." [9] Sore after doing something adds: "Go gently today and be no worse tomorrow. If you are, do less." [9]

### Neck

Get help. Call 911: the all-places line. See a clinician today: pain after an accident, a fever with a stiff neck, new pins and needles or weakness in an arm, or trouble with balance, walking or coordination. [3][11] See a clinician: it has not gone after a few weeks, painkillers have not helped, you are worried about it, or you have pins and needles or a cold arm. [3] Keep the neck moving; a collar makes it worse. [3]

Routine (five moves, sitting on a chair): [13][14][15]
1. Chin nod. Sit tall, breastbone lifted a little, shoulder blades drawn gently back. Nod your chin down as far as you can without bending the neck; only the head moves. Hold five seconds. Do five to ten. [13] For: the small muscles at the top of the neck that hold your head up.
2. Head turn. Turn your head to one side, keeping your chin at the same height. Gently tense the neck and hold five seconds, return to the middle, then the other side. Five times each side. [14] If sitting is uncomfortable, do it lying down. [14] For: turning further, more easily.
3. Head tilt. Tilt your head towards one shoulder, leading with your ear, without lifting the shoulder. Hold five seconds, return, then the other side. Five times each side. [14] For: the sides of the neck.
4. Chin to chest. Slowly tilt your head down to rest your chin on your chest, gently tense, hold five seconds. Five times. [14] For: the back of the neck.
5. Shoulder blade squeeze. Squeeze your shoulder blades back and together, hold five seconds, then let them drop down, hold five seconds. Ten times. [15] For: the upper back that carries the neck.

### Shoulders

Get help. Call 911: the all-places line, and any shoulder pain that comes with chest pain or breathlessness. [5] See a clinician today: sudden severe pain, you cannot move your arm at all, the shoulder looks out of shape or is badly swollen, pins and needles or numbness that will not go, the arm changes colour or temperature, pain after an injury, both shoulders at once, or a fever with it. [5] See a clinician: no better after two weeks, or you can barely move the arm. [5] Keep the shoulder moving gently; resting it completely slows it down. [5]

Routine (five moves, standing, then sitting at a table): [15][16]
1. Shoulder circle. Rest one hand on a chair and let the other arm hang. Swing it gently forwards and backwards, then in a small circle. About five each way, two to three times a day. [15] For: a warm-up that loosens a stiff shoulder without loading it.
2. Shoulder blade squeeze and drop. Squeeze your shoulder blades back and together, hold five seconds; pull them down, hold five seconds. Ten times. [15] For: the muscles that hold the shoulder in a good position.
3. Wall climb. Stand facing a wall with the hand of the sore side on it. Walk your fingers up the wall as high as is comfortable, then walk them back down. Five to ten times. [16] For: reaching up, with the wall taking the weight.
4. Hands-clasped lift. Clasp your hands in front of you and raise them over your head, letting your stronger side guide and help the sore one. Hold five to ten seconds at the top. Five times. [16] For: reaching overhead with help.
5. Table slide. Sit at a table with your palms on a cloth. Slide the cloth forwards, tilting from the waist with a straight back, until your arms are as straight as is comfortable. Let your head drop gently, hold five seconds, slide back. Five times. [15] For: reaching forward without strain.

### Hips

Get help. Call 911: the all-places line; severe pain after a fall or injury, you cannot walk or put weight on the leg, or tingling or loss of feeling in the hip or leg after an injury. [6] See a clinician today: sudden severe hip pain without an injury, a hip that is swollen or hot with the skin changing colour, or hip pain with a fever and feeling unwell. [6] See a clinician: it stops your normal day or sleep, it gets worse or keeps coming back, it is no better after two weeks, or the hip is stiff for more than 30 minutes after you wake up. [6] Keep moving if you can; avoid low chairs and heavy carrying for now. [6]

Routine (six moves, lying, then standing at a counter): [17][18]
1. Knee to chest. Lie on your back. Pull one knee towards your chest with the other leg straight, up to the point you feel a pull. Hold 20 to 30 seconds, relax. Two to three times each side. [17] For: the front of the hip and the lower back.
2. Knee drop-outs. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat and hip-width apart. Let one knee drop out to the side as far as is comfortable, keeping your back flat, then bring it back. Five to ten each side. [17] For: turning the hip outward.
3. Bridge. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat. Lift your hips and lower back off the floor, hold five seconds, lower slowly. Five to ten. [17] For: the muscles in your buttocks and the back of your thighs that carry the hip.
4. Standing leg back. Hold a counter or chair. Move one leg straight back, keeping the knee straight and clenching the buttock; do not lean forward. Hold five seconds. Five each side. [17] For: the muscles behind the hip.
5. Standing leg out. Hold a counter. Lift one leg straight out to the side, body upright, hold five seconds, lower slowly. Five each side. [17] For: the muscles on the outside of the hip that steady you when you walk.
6. Half-kneeling lunge. Kneel on one knee with the other foot in front. Lift the back knee slightly, look forward, and push your hips forward with your upper body upright. Hold five seconds. Three times each side. [18] For: the front of the hip that shortens from sitting.

### Knees

Get help. Call 911: the all-places line. See a clinician today: the knee is very painful, you cannot move it or put any weight on it, it is badly swollen or has changed shape, it locks, gives way or clicks painfully, or you have a high temperature with redness or heat around the knee. [4] See a clinician: no better within a few weeks. [4] Painless clicking is normal. [4]

Routine (six moves, lying, then standing; these build strength, because that is what the evidence supports for a sore knee): [10][19]
1. Thigh squeeze. Lie with the leg straight. Tighten the muscle at the front of your thigh and gently push the back of the knee down into the bed. Hold ten seconds, relax. Five to ten. [10] For: the front-of-thigh muscle that steadies the knee.
2. Heel slide. Lie with both legs straight. Slowly bend the sore knee by sliding the heel towards you as far as is comfortable, hold two seconds, slide back. Five to ten. [10] For: bending the knee further.
3. Straight leg raise. Lie with one leg bent and the sore leg straight. Lift the straight leg a few inches, hold five seconds, lower slowly. Five to ten. [10] For: strength without bending the knee.
4. Sit to stand. Stand in front of a chair. Slowly bend your knees until your bottom just touches the seat, arms out in front for balance, then stand back up. Do not sit all the way down. Five to ten. Start with a cushion on the seat if the chair is low. [10][19] For: getting out of chairs and off the toilet without pain.
5. Step up. Stand at the bottom step. Step up with the sore leg, keep it on the step, and slowly step up and down with the other leg. Hold the rail if you need it. Five to ten. [10] For: stairs.
6. Single leg stand. Stand next to a counter. Stand on the sore leg and lift the other foot off the floor. Hold a few seconds, working up to 20. Three times. [10] For: balance, which protects the knee.

### Feet and heels

Get help. Call 911: the all-places line. See a clinician today: severe heel pain after an injury, fainting or feeling sick from the pain, the foot or ankle looks out of shape, you heard a snap, grind or pop, you cannot walk, stand on tiptoe or climb stairs, or the calf and ankle are swollen and bruised. [7] See a clinician: the pain is severe or stops your normal day, it gets worse or keeps coming back, it is no better after two weeks, you have tingling or loss of feeling in the foot, or you have diabetes. [7] Wear wide, comfortable, low-heeled shoes with a soft sole; avoid long spells barefoot. [7]

Routine (six moves; the first four are the ones to do first thing in the morning, before the first steps): [20][21]
1. Calf stretch at the wall. Face a wall, hands on it at shoulder height. Put one foot behind the other, back leg straight and heel down, front knee bent, and lean in until you feel a pull in the back of the lower leg. Hold 20 to 30 seconds. Three times each side. [20] For: the muscle at the back of the lower leg that pulls on the heel.
2. Bent-knee calf stretch. The same, with the back knee slightly bent. Hold 20 to 30 seconds. Three times each side. [20] For: the deeper part of the same muscle.
3. Toe pull. Sit and cross the sore foot over the other knee. Hold your toes and gently pull them back towards the shin until you feel a pull in the arch. Hold 20 to 30 seconds. Three times. [20] For: the band under the foot that hurts in the morning.
4. Towel stretch. Sit with the leg straight, a towel looped around the ball of the foot, knee straight, and pull the towel towards you. Hold 20 seconds, rest, and repeat three times. Best first thing in the morning or after a rest. [21] For: the same band, before the day's first steps.
5. Rolling. Roll the underside of the foot over a tennis ball or a bottle for one to two minutes. [20] For: easing the sole.
6. Heel raises. Stand with feet shoulder-width apart, hand on a wall for balance. Slowly rise onto your toes, then lower. Ten to fifteen, two to three sets. [20] For: strength in the lower leg, which takes load off the heel.

### Hands and wrists

Get help. Call 911: the all-places line. See a clinician today: pain after an injury with a wrist that looks out of shape or that you cannot move, or numbness that persists. [11] See a clinician: symptoms that are getting worse or not going away, numbness or tingling that wakes you at night, or a home approach that is not working after a few weeks. [8] A night splint is the first thing a clinician usually suggests for tingling fingers; wearing one can take up to six weeks to help. [8]

Routine (six moves, sitting at a table): [22]
1. Tendon glides. Start with the fingers straight. Make a hook (bend the fingertips and middle joints, knuckles straight), open; make a full fist, open; make a flat fist (fingers bent at the knuckles, straight beyond), open. Five to ten rounds. For: letting the finger cords slide freely.
2. Thumb across. Hold your hand up as if saying stop. Move the thumb across the palm to the base of the little finger, then back. Five to ten. For: the thumb's reach.
3. Hand lift. Rest the forearm on the table with the hand over the edge, palm down. Lift the hand up until you feel a gentle pull, hold 20 to 30 seconds, return. Two to three times. For: the top of the forearm.
4. Palm turn. Elbow bent to a right angle, turn the forearm so the palm faces up, then down. Five to ten. For: turning the wrist, like a key.
5. Wave. Forearm on the table or knee with a towel under it, thumb up. Move the wrist up and down as if waving. Five to ten. For: the wrist's full range.
6. Towel bunch. Palm flat on a towel on the table, fingers spread. Press down and bunch the towel between the fingers, then spread them apart. Five to ten. For: grip.

## Open questions

- Whether "Sore, after doing something" should show a shorter routine than "Stiff", rather than the same routine with a different line. Left as the same routine for the first version; his call after using it.
- Whether a per-move timer earns its place. Nothing here needs one; a 20 to 30 second hold can be counted.
- Upper back, elbows and ankles are not covered. They can be added with the same shape once a source is chosen for each.
